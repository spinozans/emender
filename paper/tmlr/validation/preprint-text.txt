Emending Nonlinear Recurrence
Erik Garrison erik.garrison@gmail.com
Poietic PBC / UTHSC, Memphis, TN, USA
Abstract
Conversation, perception, and feedback call for ongoing computation with predictable per-
input cost. Emender explores a complementary path through eﬃcient nonlinear recurrence
within bounded memory: each input corrects the memory’s current prediction and trans-
forms the state carried forward. Width-axis multi-programming runs many small memory
heads in parallel while preserving each head’s sequential update. At 1.3 billion parameters,
E97 trains through 150.8 billion Pile tokens on one eight-GPU workstation and reaches com-
parable training loss to a tightly parameter-matched GDN2 control. On the trained pair,
modular-quadratic terminal accuracy is 99.96% versus 76.09% at trained length, and 76.07%
versus 53.36% at eight times that length. This is a single-seed, common-cap comparison with
unequal realized training over the tested lengths. On S5 group-state tracking, GDN2 leads
the long-horizon comparison. Lean 4 results describe the underlying state computations:
E97 applies one tanh-of-aﬃne state update per token, while a linear-state GDN fold under
a ﬁxed, state-independent parameter schedule is aﬃne in its initial state, with zero nonlin-
ear temporal depth. The artifacts also establish matched-signature delta-versus-raw-write
separation and an S5 routing bridge; minimal circuit-depth lower bounds and a full-stack im-
possibility remain open. The practical training result and iterated-map advantage support
repeated nonlinear state transformation as a useful computational bias. Feedback, optimiza-
tion, reservoirs, and dynamics deﬁne prospective structural applications. Recurrent tracker
realization and constrained aﬃne-channel realizability are explicit research targets.

expressivity · Lean 4
1 Introduction
Keeping balance, following a moving object, and adjusting a movement require continual revision of an
internal estimate as observations arrive. Conversation and perception likewise require a model to update
what it understands while the interaction unfolds. Fast feedback puts this computation under a per-input
time budget. A complementary question is how much of this ongoing computation can happen directly in
carried memory as inputs arrive. Eﬃcient nonlinear recurrence oﬀers such a path: at inference, a ﬁxed-width,
ﬁxed-depth model transforms ﬁxed-size state with bounded work per update.
A sequence can require more than a record of what arrived. Its elements may be instructions that change
a running calculation, measurements that alter a feedback response, or observations that revise an earlier
association. In each case the current state is part of the next computation. A recurrent model reads an input,
updates its internal state, and carries that state into the following step. The question is what computation
the update performs, and how to train it eﬃciently at language-model scale.
This choice creates a systems problem. Modern linear-state recurrences use the composability of aﬃne
state updates to parallelize training along time (Gu & Dao, 2024) (Dao & Gu, 2024) (Yang et al., 2024a)
(Hatamizadeh et al., 2026). E97 keeps each head’s nonlinear time loop serial and obtains parallelism across
heads and batch elements. We call this width-axis multi-programming: many small memory programs run
side by side, with each state tile kept on the accelerator (Section 5). This complements hybrid recurrent-
attention stacks and iterative methods for parallelizing nonlinear recurrence (Mishra et al., 2026) (Danieli
et al., 2025).

The resulting program joins a practical architecture to a testable computational hypothesis. Feedback
systems, iterative optimization, and driven dynamics provide further settings in which a running state must
be transformed repeatedly (Section 8).
The ﬁrst empirical question is whether this route is practical. A purely nonlinear recurrent language model
— E97, the current 1.3 B-class instance of the Emender family — made trainable by running many small
recurrent programs in parallel across the network’s width rather than across time, trains through 150.8 billion
tokens on The Pile (Gao et al., 2020) using a single eight-GPU workstation, with no cluster and no sequence
parallelism, reaching an estimated 0.93 bits per byte (Section 6). Its linear-recurrent baseline — GDN2
(Gated DeltaNet-2 (Hatamizadeh et al., 2026)), a mixer followed by the same SwiGLU MLP, selected by the
same CMA-ES (Hansen et al., 2003) search protocol at a parameter count within 0.01% — trains on literally
matched tokens under the same 2k-chunk Pile recipe and lands in the same loss-versus-tokens band: at the
matched 150.79-billion-token cutoﬀ the smoothed loss gap between the two arms is 0.010 nats/token, with
the sign of the gap depending on the averaging window (Section 6). The 1.3 B result establishes training
viability for both update rules after per-family tuning at this scale; loss parity and probe ordering at 7 B and
beyond remain open (Section 10). On bulk training-log loss the two update families are operationally tied,
so language-modeling loss does not separate them. Their diﬀerences emerge instead in state tracking. At
8 M scale, the delta-correcting update learns the S5 preﬁx-product task more eﬃciently than the raw-write
and GDN baselines under the tested protocols. On the current 1.3 B pair, E97 leads the modular-quadratic
probe, while GDN2 leads long-horizon S5 tracking (Section 7). The Lean core proves matched-signature
one-step separation, exact non-equality at every ﬁnite k on a constructed witness, and a 120-state recognizer
ﬂoor. Its generator-table lookup construction proves the lookup bridge; a theorem that the tanh recurrence
implements the full tracker is the trajectory bridge still open as Open item O1 (Section 4). The duality
and the cited complexity results hold at their stated resource classes; a blanket linear-state S5 impossibility
remains open.
The second question is what these trained models can learn to compute. An iterated nonlinear map re-
peatedly transforms a running value, directly testing the computational pattern that motivates E97. After
ﬁne-tuning on a modular-quadratic recurrence, E97 reaches 99.96% versus 76.09% accuracy at the trained
length and 76.07% versus 53.36% at eight times that length. Its higher accuracy and greater length robust-
ness support nonlinear transformation in the carried-state path as a useful inductive bias. The S5 group-state
task instead asks the models to compose permutation actions. GDN2 leads that long-horizon comparison.
Section 7 gives the protocols, learning trajectories, and length-dependent results for both tasks.
Emender uses a nonlinear matrix memory. At each step, a head reads its current prediction at an input-
supplied key, writes a correction toward the supplied value, and passes the updated state through tanh. The
write uses what the memory already contains; the nonlinearity transforms what the next step will receive.
Processing a long sequence therefore executes a long composition of nonlinear state updates.
An emender is one recurrent layer, and Emender is the family of networks formed by stacking these layers.
The current instance, E97, uses separate key-axis erase/read and value-axis write gates. Each head has a
small matrix state, while projections, gates, and a SwiGLU MLP connect the memories to the rest of the
network (Section 3). The memory is revisable: later inputs can correct earlier associations.
Tanh-with-latching complements delta correction. Under the hypotheses of theorem set F (Section 4), a
saturated slot resists perturbations, keeps its sign below a write threshold, and releases under a suﬃcient
counter-delta. These are conditional slot-level guarantees; the hold theorem EmenderLatching.emender_
latch_holds_by_default assumes λ ≥ 1, while production decay sits below one. Persistent, revisable
memory is the design motivation; a proved default persistence behavior of the trained stack remains open.
E88 is the developmental predecessor (Section A).

Delta correction is one response; hybrids are another
The recurrent state-tracking problem admits more than one architectural response. Hybrid architectures
interleave recurrent layers with attention layers and recover much of what attention-free recurrence loses on
state tracking and long-range mixing. M2
RNN (Mishra et al., 2026), OLMo-Hybrid (Merrill et al., 2026),
Titans (Behrouz et al., 2025), and Griﬃn (De et al., 2024) are recent instances, and the limit-of-pure-
recurrence story is documented by Merrill, Petty and Sabharwal (Merrill et al., 2024). Delta correction is
a diﬀerent response. It changes what the recurrent state writes at each step from a raw additive overwrite
to a correction against the existing slot. The two responses are complementary: a hybrid stack can use any
update rule in its recurrent layers, and delta correction is well-deﬁned wherever a matrix-state recurrent
layer is.
We evaluate delta correction in the attention-free, time-serial recurrent arena, the pure nonlinear recurrent
(PNR) setting in the sense of "no cross-token attention mechanism"; gating, projections, normalizations
and other intra-token nonlinear operations are permitted and load-bearing (Section 3 ingredient (d) and
Section 6 on gradient conditioning). The PNR arena is where the contrast is cleanest (a delta-vs-raw-write
contrast in the speciﬁed update-resource class, Section 4). Set D uses synthetic state-pass charges, excluding
actual dense HW and feature-network costs; actual matched-compute certiﬁcation remains open. The Lean
4 separation theorems are tractable in this arena (sets C and C′
), and the scaling demonstration trains this
attention-free, time-serial architecture directly.
The two recent attempts to scale nonlinear recurrence each took a diﬀerent concession. M2
RNN (Mishra
et al., 2026) trains nonlinear matrix-state recurrence at 7 B MoE in hybrid form (nonlinear-recurrent layers
interleaved with attention layers); ParaRNN (Danieli et al., 2025) trains nonlinear-recurrent LSTM and
GRU at 7 B by parallelizing the time loop via Newton iteration on a block-bidiagonal Jacobian. These
routes use attention or time-axis parallelization. This work takes the pure-recurrent route and compares a
delta-correcting instance directly with a raw-write instance under matched conditions.
M2
RNN already identiﬁes the same basic hardware fact: a nonlinear recurrence cannot be scanned across
time, so useful parallelism must come from batch and head parallelism rather than from the time axis. The
diﬀerence here is how far that principle is pushed and what it is used to test. M2
RNN promotes nonlinear
matrix recurrence mainly as a sparse ingredient in hybrid stacks; under the addressing-bottleneck hypothesis,
the pure setting may beneﬁt from many independent address programs at a given value-head count — what
we call address-program multiplicity. The CMA-reshaped raw-write comparator used here moves M2
RNN
into that same pure multi-programmed regime and is stable where the paper-default shape diverged. This
controls the broad pure-recurrent systems setting for comparing raw overwrite with delta correction; width,
depth, and head count also changed, leaving the cause of divergence unresolved.

2 Background
The central distinction is where nonlinear computation occurs. Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2024) and Gated
DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2024a) use nonlinear input processing and gates, and their full networks are nonlinear
functions of the sequence. Once a recurrent layer’s input-derived transition parameters are ﬁxed, its carried-
state update is aﬃne in the previous state. E97 instead transforms the carried state nonlinearly at each
step. These choices organize computation across time diﬀerently and call for diﬀerent execution strategies.
The stakes are the general ones of sequence modeling at scale, and one workload makes them concrete.
Pangenomic sequence data runs to terabases per study (Liao et al., 2023) (Guarracino et al., 2023) (Gar-
rison et al., 2024), so modeling it at the byte level means ingesting trillions of tokens for any downstream
operation, which rules out routine use. Linear-recurrent byte-level foundation models were the ﬁrst at-
tempt in this regime and did not scale reliably. The Merrill–Petty–Sabharwal results (Merrill et al., 2024)
give conditional circuit-containment results for restricted linear SSMs under their transition and arithmetic
assumptions. A non-solvable-state-tracking impossibility would additionally require the open complexity-
class separation. This motivated studying nonlinear-in-time updates as an empirical research direction; the
Emender of Section 3 is the construction evaluated here.

Linear-state and nonlinear-state recurrence
The recurrent language model lineage runs from Elman networks (Elman, 1990) and the LSTM (Hochreiter
& Schmidhuber, 1997) through the modern linear-recurrent variants: state-space models such as Mamba
and Mamba2 (Gu & Dao, 2024) (Dao & Gu, 2024), gated linear attention (Yang et al., 2023), and the delta
rule (Yang et al., 2024b). Many modern designs favor time-axis parallelism via linear-state updates, whereas
nonlinear-in-time recurrence generally uses a diﬀerent execution strategy. Gated DeltaNet-2 (Hatamizadeh
et al., 2026), the split erase/write-gate extension of Gated DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2024a), supplies the linear-
state mixer in the current GDN2 control (Section 6).
Gated DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2024a) is the linear-recurrent family the Section 7 8 M probes use as the linear
baseline, and Gated DeltaNet-2 (Hatamizadeh et al., 2026) — its split erase/write-gate extension — is the
linear-recurrent baseline of the 1.3 B matched-token comparison (Section 6). Mamba-3 (Lahoti et al., 2026)
is reported by its authors to outperform GDN; its compilation-heavy HPO does not ﬁt the Section 6 matched
protocol, so it is left to future work.
A single explicit criterion classiﬁes recurrent architectures. A recurrent layer is linear-state if its update can
be written St = AtSt−1 + bt with At and bt determined by input-derived features, independently of that
layer’s previous carried state. Conditioning on these parameters makes the update aﬃne in St−1. Here the
state is expressed in vector coordinates; a matrix state may be vectorized for this criterion. If the previous
state St−1 appears nonlinearly in its own update equation, the layer is nonlinear-state. The distinction is
structural across activation choices: a tanh, an exponential, a tanh-of-product, or a sigmoid.
Linear-state designs admit a chunkwise or scan-based parallel implementation along the time axis because
composing ST = AT ST −1 + bT = . . . unfolds into a product of inputs only. This is the reason linear-state
recurrence dominates the billion-parameter landscape. Mamba, Mamba2 (SSD), RetNet, GLA, DeltaNet,
Gated DeltaNet, RWKV-4/5/6/7, HGRN2, mLSTM, MinGRU/MinLSTM (Feng et al., 2024) and Griﬃn’s
RG-LRU (De et al., 2024) are all linear-state by this criterion. At ﬁxed precision and width, both state
regimes are ﬁnite-state recognizers. Circuit-complexity containment results for restricted linear SSMs re-
quire additional arithmetic and transition assumptions (Merrill et al., 2024). Containment in TC0

The S5 state-tracking probe
The symmetric group S5 is the group of permutations of ﬁve items; it has 120 elements and is the smallest non-
solvable symmetric group. Its word problem is the following task: the model reads a sequence of permutations
— here, adjacent transpositions, the swaps of two neighboring items — one at a time, and after each one
must report the running composition of everything read so far, its preﬁx product. By Barrington’s theorem
(Barrington, 1986) this task is complete for the complexity class NC1
when exact recognition is required
over all words. The experiments measure preﬁx-product accuracy at speciﬁed lengths. The solvable-group
control S3 (permutations of three items, 6 elements) is included to factor out the part of diﬃculty that comes
from preﬁx tracking per se rather than from non-solvability. Training-length learning failure is an empirical
observation about SGD, separate from circuit-class membership.
A dynamics taxonomy of head-types: eigenvalue placement × saturation
The linear/nonlinear hinge above, and the choice of where a head’s state may decay, reﬂect, or oscillate, are
two independent axes of a single design grid. The dynamics of a head’s per-token state map locate it in a
two-axis grid:
1. Eigenvalue placement in the unit disk. Where the along-key eigenvalue of the per-token state transi-
tion sits in the complex unit disk. A real-positive eigenvalue is a decay — the stored value fades to-
ward zero along the key. A real-negative eigenvalue is a reﬂection that ﬂips sign each step (reflect);
reaching it is the lever that lets a recurrence track non-solvable group structure such as S5 in the
restricted transition analyses motivating this grid. A complex-conjugate pair is a rotation (rot) that
oscillates the stored value. Real-positive and real-negative are the two ends of the real diameter;
complex placement walks oﬀ that diameter into the rest of the disk.
2. State map: linear or saturated. Whether the state update is left linear or passed through a bounded
saturating nonlinearity (canonically hardtanh; the -nonlin axis; the Emender uses the smooth
variant tanh). Saturation latches a driven slot and makes the head bounded; ﬁnite precision then
gives a ﬁnite state space.
The grid these two axes span names every Emender head-type (Figure 1). Real × linear at the positive end
is decay, which is vanilla Gated DeltaNet; at the negative end it is reflect, Gated DeltaNet permitted a
negative eigenvalue. Real × saturated is nonlin — the saturated delta-correcting head, historically tagged
E97, whose split-gated instance is the 1.3 B model of this paper with split erase/read and write gates.
Complex × linear is rot, the complex-eigenvalue oscillator head. Complex × saturated is rot-nonlin,
a nonlinear oscillator left to future work, included as the combination of the two axes. E97 identiﬁes the
trained instance; the architecture vocabulary is this grid.

Parameterization choices
E97 parameterizes the per-head decay dh in log space, following Mamba2 (Dao & Gu, 2024): each head
learns a scalar Alog and a bias δbias — the code names A_log and dt_bias — and forms the decay as
d = exp(− exp(Alog) · softplus(α(x) + δbias)), where α(x) is the input-dependent term and σ is again the
logistic sigmoid. Both decay parameters are exempt from weight decay, as is standard for such timescale
parameters. The recurrence is computed in ﬂoat32 and cast back to the storage precision, and the tanh on
the state uses the numerically stable form tanh(z) = 2σ(2z)−1 in the fused kernel to avoid overﬂow at large
pre-activations. Three remaining choices are: there is no output normalization inside the recurrent layer —
an ablation removed it for a 0.10-nat gain, and the wrapper’s block-level pre-norm plus ﬁnal norm suﬃce
— no short convolution on the input. E97 uses the split erase/read and write gates above; its output gate
silu(g) still acts on the read alone.
The 1.3 B stack
Ingredient (d) sets the 1.3 B stack’s shape — many small heads — and the numbers follow from it: each
head’s 32 × 32 tile is small enough to live in registers (Section 5), the head count H = 216 is large enough
to give that many independent addressing programs per layer, and eleven such layers — each followed by its
SwiGLU MLP, the channel-mixer that pairs with the token-mixing recurrence — reach the 1.3 B-class budget
the paired comparison ﬁxes. The exact values were selected by the per-architecture search of Section 6. At
batch size 4, depth 11 and H = 216 multiply to 11 × 216 × 4 = 9{, }504 independent recurrent programs per
token, each carrying its own 32×32 state tile. The reference implementation and the fused Triton recurrence
kernel that steps these tiles are identiﬁed by the source register and release manifests.
The matched architectural contrast
E97 and GDN2 both use matrix memory, delta correction, split editing gates, and a SwiGLU channel-mixing
MLP. The key distinction for the present hypothesis is the operation performed on carried state: E97 applies
tanh after its correction, whereas the GDN2 state update is aﬃne when its input-supplied parameters are
ﬁxed. Decay placement also diﬀers, as the core identities above specify. The full networks contain nonlinear
projections, gates, and MLPs. The comparison centers on the transformation applied to carried state. The
matched-pair probes test what the trained architectures learn under the recorded protocol (Section 7); the
formal analysis states the narrower update-map facts (Section 4).

Theorem set A: ﬁnite-state ceiling and S5 tracker
At ﬁxed width and precision the Emender is a ﬁnite-state machine, and the S5 word problem it is tested on
is a concrete, fully speciﬁed ﬁnite-state target.
• Finite-state ceiling at ﬁxed precision. At ﬁxed width and precision, every online recognizer of this kind
has only ﬁnitely many distinct states (S5Witness.fixed_precision_state_space_finite). This gives a
ﬁnite online state space.
• S5 word problem. The target is pinned down exactly in Lean. The group S5 has 120 elements (S5Witn
ess.s5_state_count) and is non-solvable (S5Witness.s5_not_solvable); the preﬁx-product tracker that
reads a word of transpositions and reports the running product is a 120-state recognizer (S5Tracker.reco
gnizer_state_count) whose state composes correctly as tokens are appended (S5Tracker.run_append).
A bridge lemma conﬁrms the Lean tracker agrees with the Python evaluation harness on every input (S5Tr
acker.pythonRun_eq_tracker_tuple). The four adjacent transpositions generate all of S5: the tracker’s
run map is surjective (S5Inseparability.run_surjective, via Mathlib’s Equiv.Perm.mclosure_swap_c
astSucc_succ).
• Capacity lower bound (recognizer ﬂoor). Any ﬁnite-state recognizer with a state decoder that reproduces
the tracker on every input word needs at least 120 states (S5Inseparability.capacity_lower_bound_120);
equivalently, no recognizer with fewer than 120 states can realize it under any decoder (S5Inseparabili
ty.small_recognizer_cannot_realize_s5). The bound has a distinguishing-pair form: any recognizer
with too few states admits two words it cannot tell apart that the tracker does distinguish (S5Insepara
bility.exists_distinguishing_word_pair). This is the existential form of the S5-coset inseparability
target: it holds for any ﬁnite recognizer family, with no architectural assumptions. The recognizer ﬂoor is
the state-counting lemma required by a future bounded-precision raw-write bound.
• Lookup-table realization. The tracker is small enough to write down as a table: it uses exactly 120×4 =
480 state/input keys (S5NDMRealization.s5_transition_key_count), and any ﬁnite recognizer admits
such an exact lookup-table realization (S5NDMRealization.exactTransitionMemory_run).
Theorem set B: generator-table lookup and the recurrent-tracker target
The exact statement of NDMRealizesS5.emender_realizes_s5_tracker supplies d = 12, four orthonormal
keys and one-hot values, queries, a decoder, and λ = 1. Reading a preloaded table retrieves the current
generator; decoding it and externally multiplying a supplied S5 state s reproduces the transition s · g. The
linear readout supplies the generator value, and the external multiplication updates the group state. The
supporting OnlineMemory.linearDeltaWrite_overwrites_one_preserves_others is an exact pre-tanh
overwrite statement.
A recurrent realization would carry the running group product in its own state.
Open item O1 — recurrent tanh-tracker realization unavailable. The loaded generator memory does not
carry the running group product; external composition supplies the missing state computation. Closing O1
requires a conﬁguration of the actual nonlinear update, an explicit initial state and decoder, and an all-words
trajectory theorem. This is a stronger, currently unproved architecture claim, beyond the already-proved
lookup identity.

5 Multi-Programming and Systems
The formal results establish the mechanism; the next question is whether a serial nonlinear recurrence can
be trained to competitive scale at all.
Multi-programming: the throughput-enabling design choice
Width-axis multi-programming supplies throughput while each head’s time loop remains serial. A linear
recurrence earns its throughput on the time axis: because St = AtSt−1 + bt unfolds into a product that
depends on the inputs alone, the step-by-step loop can be re-expressed as a preﬁx scan or a chunkwise
matrix multiplication over the whole sequence at once. E97 executes its nonlinear state update in a serial
time loop. Multi-programming obtains throughput on the other axis by replicating the recurrent computation
across many independent heads — each with its own small bounded matrix state — and harvests parallelism
19
across those heads, across state tiles, and across batch elements, while the time loop inside each head still
runs one step at a time.
The design choice is a decoupling: it frees throughput from the shape of the update. The recurrent body
must stay a bounded, register-resident per-step map at a ﬁxed matrix-state signature — that is what keeps
each head cheap. This implementation supports a programmable nonlinear update body. The cost is paid
in per-head serial time, most exposed at long sequence lengths where the serial loop is longest; the gain is
that a nonlinear recurrence can keep a modern accelerator busy with no time-axis scan at all.
The route is agnostic to the update rule: both pure-nonlinear instances of the predecessor cohort — the
Emender and the raw-write M2
RNN-CMA — have geometries consistent with the same simple resource
predicate. The named Lean theorem RecurrentResourceFormalism.multiProgrammed_admits_m2rn
n_and_emender checks E88 and a diﬀerent M2
RNN proxy (depth 22, 70 heads, 256 state scalars/head),
not the trained depth-21, 370-head M2
RNN-CMA. The latter satisﬁes the four signature-ﬁeld conditions by
inspection/arithmetic, not by this exact-instance theorem (Section 4).
The 1.3 B Emender shape under multi-programming
A modern GPU exposes many independent streaming multiprocessors, each able to run a small program out
of its own registers and shared memory, and multi-programming is built to ﬁll them. E97, the 1.3 B Emender
instance, runs 216 such programs per layer per batch element — one per head — so at depth 11 and batch
size 4 the layer stack issues 11 × 216 × 4 = 9{, }504 small independent recurrent programs per token, each
carrying its own 32 × 32 state tile small enough to live in registers. Keeping the accelerator busy therefore
uses parallelism across memory programs. The paired throughput and memory measurements below test
the practical consequence at the actual E97/GDN2 geometries; direct E97 occupancy proﬁling remains to
be measured.
Measured throughput and utilization (CUDA only)
All throughput measurements and throughput-budget smokes reported here use CUDA; paired ROCm cor-
rectness, performance, and parity measurements remain pending.
At the actual 1.3 B pair geometries, the matched-batch preﬂight on seven RTX 6000 Ada GPUs under DDP
measures E97-MLP at 3,211 tokens/s per GPU and GDN2 at 3,290, both at batch 4. The ratio is 0.976:
comparable throughput, with GDN2 slightly faster. E97 uses 28,942 MB peak memory per GPU against
35,715 MB for GDN2. E97 therefore ﬁts batch 6 under DDP, reaching 4,470 tokens/s per GPU, where
the control cannot ﬁt that batch (experiments/preflight_100b/RESULTS.md). The lower memory use
enables the larger-batch throughput gain. The small grok-scale study measured a 1.26–1.56× speedup; the
matched-batch 1.3 B preﬂight measures a ratio of 0.976.
The preﬂight uses DDP at the actual pair geometries. The long-run training below uses eight independent
local-DiLoCo islands. Parameter count, measured throughput, and peak memory are the reported resource
comparisons; measuring each implementation’s actual FLOPs is a further systems experiment.
Predecessor-cohort occupancy, MFU, and the linear-scan kernel comparison are recorded in Section A.
Fused Triton recurrence kernel
The forward kernel uses an internal [T,B,H,*] layout (time, batch, head, . . .) and dispatches one program
per (batch,head_block). The BLOCK_H parameter is autotuned over {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} because at H ≥ 256
per-program-per-head launch overhead dominates. The state tile lives in registers and shared memory for
the duration of the T-step loop. The following pieces are fused into the same Triton program: tanh/silu
activations on input projections, L2
normalization of q, k, the recurrent delta write, and the output gate.
Each fusion removes one to two torch.cuda launches per layer call; at depth 12 the aggregate saving is
approximately 50–60 ms per step.
20
Sparse-checkpoint backward
The forward kernel saves S only every K = 16 steps. The backward kernel processes one K-step segment
at a time: it forward-replays the K steps to rebuild per-step St−1 from the saved checkpoint, then walks
backward to apply the chain rule. The activation memory used by the kernel is T
K + 1 checkpoint slots
instead of T. At K = 16 this is approximately a 16× shrink, at the cost of a single extra forward pass per
segment.
Triton source portability; CUDA-only validation
The recurrence is implemented in Triton (Tillet et al., 2019). The paired production runs, throughput
measurements, and expressivity smokes use NVIDIA CUDA. Porting the pair to ROCm would add a direct
test of correctness and performance on that backend. The successor E97 Frontier continuation is a separate
single-arm study; paired backend evidence remains pending.
Distributed training
The 1.3 B-class result reported here was produced on a single eight-GPU workstation, with no cluster and
no sequence parallelism — both 150 B-token arms of Section 6 ran the same local DiLoCo recipe on that one
machine: eight independent islands (one per GPU), schedule-free AdamW (Defazio et al., 2024) inside each
island, and plain model-weight averaging across islands every τ = 250 local steps (outer learning rate 1.0,
momentum 0 — the conservative plain-average form of DiLoCo (Douillard et al., 2023)). Scaling the same
recipe past one machine pairs schedule-free AdamW per island with hierarchical local-SGD averaging: each
island is one node of 8 GPUs with intra-island DDP, and inter-island synchronization averages model weights
on the same τ = 250 cadence. The structural point holds either way: because the Emender distributes work
across programs, the 1.3 B implementation uses ordinary head and batch parallelism while preserving each
head’s time loop.
ParaRNN (Danieli et al., 2025) oﬀers a diﬀerent systems route, using an iterative solver to parallelize
nonlinear recurrence along time. The present implementation instead retains one serial pass per head and
obtains its parallelism across heads and batch elements.
In the implementation study, we tried it on the tanh(dS + kδT
) map at 32 × 32 block size and found it
markedly slower in throughput than the multi-programmed Triton kernels above. Newton iteration carries a
data-dependent solve count per step, and its convergence on the bounded tanh map is unestablished, whereas
multi-programming is a single serial pass per head.

7 Expressivity Results
Comparable language-modeling loss leaves open what the carried state can learn to compute. We ﬁne-tune
the same 1.3 B E97 and GDN2 checkpoints on two explicit computations. The modular-quadratic task
repeatedly applies a nonlinear map to a running value; E97’s advantage supports the mechanism hypothesis
that motivates its state update. The S5 task composes ﬁnite-group actions; GDN2’s strong result shows
a contrasting learned capability on group actions. Both studies concern learned performance under stated
budgets and protocols.
State-count headroom at 8 M parameters
State-tracking probes in this section run at 8 M parameter-matched scale (dim = 384, depth = 4, H = 32,
N = 32, schedule-free AdamW, 10K–20K steps per task, three seeds; GDN uses dim = 640 to match
parameter count). The S5 and S3 transition tables have information-theoretic ﬂoors of log2120 ≈ 6.9 bits
and log26 ≈ 2.6 bits; an 8 M-parameter model exceeds those ﬂoors by roughly seven orders of magnitude
in parameter bits and ﬁve in recurrent-state scalars per token. These counts rule out fewer-than-120-state
storage as the explanation; constrained-map expressivity and optimization remain candidate bottlenecks.
A learning-failure interpretation is a hypothesis supported by this headroom and the 1.3 B parallel-decay
boundary result; deriving it from the lookup theorem remains Open item O1 (Section 4). The probes measure
what SGD ﬁnds under the stated no-tuning protocol.
Probe defaults and the state-tracking selection asymmetry
The 8 M probe scale received no probe-speciﬁc hyperparameter search and no seed sweep for any family in
the comparison. The Emender ran on the default conﬁguration carried down from its 1.3 B stack (the E88-
lineage default; the E97 instance of Section 6 postdates these probes); M2
RNN-CMA ran on the analogous
default from its CMA-tuned reshape; GDN and the M2
RNN-paper shape ran on their respective published
defaults. Each family is therefore evaluated on the reasonable-defaults conﬁguration it would arrive at
without probe-targeted optimization, rather than matched-after-HPO. With large state-count headroom and
no probe-speciﬁc tuning, the gaps are consistent with an inductive-bias explanation, but do not uniquely
identify it.
That matched-no-tuning condition controls probe-speciﬁc eﬀort; the defaults retain a selection-criterion
asymmetry. The Emender’s defaults are the endpoint of an ablation lineage ranked on language-modeling
loss — the same objective GDN’s and M2
RNN’s authors used for their published defaults — with one
exception: the state nonlinearity (tanh versus a linear state) was descriptively close to the linear variant on
language-modeling loss and was kept on state-tracking grounds. That one component is load-bearing for
these probes, so the Emender’s conﬁguration was settled partly on a proxy of the very quantity the probes

Interpreting the current-pair evidence
The iterated-map result is the direct empirical test of the motivating mechanism: E97’s nonlinear state
path repeatedly transforms the value that will enter the next update. Its measured advantage is consistent
with that hypothesis, while optimization, geometry, and checkpoint selection remain possible contributors.
The S5 result demonstrates that GDN2 learns a diﬀerent carried-state computation strongly. Together
with comparable language-modeling loss, these measurements make the useful question concrete: which
computations beneﬁt from placing the nonlinearity directly in the temporal state path, and under what
training and resource conditions?

9 Related Work
Ancestry: the fast-weight line on delta correction
The Emender update belongs to a long line that treats memory as a structure updated by prediction er-
ror. The earliest formulation is Widrow and Hoﬀ’s least-mean-square rule (Widrow & Hoﬀ, 1960), which
writes the error between target and current output back into the weight vector of an adaptive linear ele-
ment. Schmidhuber recast the same idea in a recurrent setting under the name fast-weight programmers
36
(Schmidhuber, 1992): a slow controller emits keys and values that update a fast-weight matrix online, with
the update being a correction against what the matrix currently predicts at the addressed slot. Schlag,
Irie, and Schmidhuber made the bridge to modern attention explicit (Schlag et al., 2021), showing that
linear-transformer kernels with cumulative outer-product updates are fast-weight programmers with a raw
additive write, and proposing the delta-rule write as a stability and capacity ﬁx. DeltaNet (Yang et al.,
2024b) carried the delta rule into a parallelizable linear-recurrent language model, demonstrating the rule
at billion-parameter scale in the linear-state regime. Emender combines delta correction with a nonlinear
matrix-state update and width-axis multi-programming. Its saturating update also motivates the conditional
latching analysis in Section 4. The hold result assumes λ ≥ 1 plus a write budget; production persistence
remains conditional.
Linear-state recurrent language models
The dominant comparison cohort is the family of linear-state billion-parameter recurrent LMs surveyed in
Section 2: Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2024) and Mamba2 (Dao & Gu, 2024) (selective SSMs); RetNet (Sun et al.,
2023) (decayed linear attention); GLA (Yang et al., 2023) (gated linear attention); DeltaNet (Yang et al.,
2024b) (delta rule, linear in S); Gated DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2024a) (gated delta rule); RWKV-4/5/6/7 (Peng
et al., 2023) (Peng et al., 2025) (linear-state WKV / generalized delta rule with linear state); HGRN2 (Qin
et al., 2024) (gated linear RNN with state expansion); MinGRU/MinLSTM (Feng et al., 2024) (input-only
gates, linear scan); mLSTM/xLSTM-7B (Beck et al., 2025) (covariance update); Griﬃn/RecurrentGemma
(De et al., 2024) (RG-LRU, often hybridized with local attention). Circuit-containment results for restricted
SSM families require their stated transition and arithmetic assumptions (Merrill et al., 2024). The current
empirical comparison uses GDN2, which leads the long-horizon S5 result in Section 7.
In the broader SSM/sequence-model lineage, S5 (Smith et al., 2022) introduces simpliﬁed state-space layers
with a multi-input multi-output linear state-space recurrence. It is distinct from the permutation group S5
used as a probe here; the cited S5 results concern sequence models, not billion-parameter recurrent LMs.
A newer linear-state result, Gated DeltaNet-2 (Hatamizadeh et al., 2026), decouples channel-wise erase and
write gates and is reported by its authors to outperform GDN. It extends retrieval and state-tracking behavior
inside the linear-state envelope, and it is the linear-recurrent baseline of this paper’s 1.3 B pairing: the GDN2
arm of Section 6 is the oﬃcial-style Gated DeltaNet-2 mixer (external source commit 95709fc) with the
same SwiGLU MLP as E97, selected by the same CMA-ES protocol. The E97 split gates of Section 3 are
the Emender’s corresponding edit — key-axis erase/read plus value-axis write — inside a nonlinear state
map, so the two architectures are adjacent by design (Section 4, set G). Its decoupled gates are a natural
next ablation for the multi-programmed substrate, as a bounded edit to the per-step update body.

11 Conclusion
Emender makes recurrent memory a site of ongoing computation. Each input corrects the stored state
and transforms what the next step receives. Running many small heads in parallel makes this sequential
computation practical: E97 trains through 150.8 B Pile tokens at 1.3 B parameters and reaches comparable
training loss to GDN2. The estimated training-log normalization is 0.93 bpb; the current pair’s held-out
evaluation is still unavailable (Section 6).
The iterated nonlinear-map experiment connects that practicality to the architectural motivation. E97 learns
the recurrence more accurately and retains a larger fraction of its accuracy as sequence length grows. GDN2
learns the distinct S5 group-state task strongly and leads its long-horizon comparison. The formal state-
channel results make the underlying computational distinction precise: repeated nonlinear composition in
E97, and an aﬃne GDN fold under a ﬁxed, state-independent schedule.
The modular-quadratic comparison uses one seed, a common cap, unequal realized training, and graceful
length transfer (Section 7). Real-valued update-family separation and the 120-state recognizer ﬂoor describe
representational scope; learning is a separate empirical question. The generator-table lookup leaves recurrent
tracker realization open (O1), and aﬃne-channel S5 realizability remains open (G3).
These ﬁndings support studying nonlinear memory as a computational resource. The next step is to test that
resource across training budgets, scales, and tasks in which an evolving state must keep being transformed:
feedback, iterative optimization, and driven dynamics.
Repeated nonlinear maps in engineering and dynamics (Section 8) deﬁne prospective structural applications.

12 Testable Predictions
The results above suggest concrete predictions, most of them testable at the next scale step or under modest
additional training. Each is stated as a claim with its scope and the observation that would falsify it, so
that later training rounds can settle them.
• Width-axis multi-programming scales beyond 1.3 B without throughput collapse. The multi-
programmed substrate harvests throughput from many small bounded heads rather than from time-
axis linearization; the per-head register/SRAM working set is independent of total parameter count,
so the recipe is expected to survive scale-up to the 3–7 B band. Falsiﬁed if per-token throughput
degrades faster than the parameter count grows, beyond the kernel-arithmetic baseline.
• The Emender’s state-tracking advantage persists at scaled training budgets. The Section 7 S5 and S3
gaps are at the 8 M parameter-matched probe scale under matched no-tuning, and the iterated-map
separation is at grok scale. At longer training-token budgets at 1.3 B and beyond, the within-PNR
ordering and the Emender-vs-GDN2 gap on state-tracking probes should hold qualitatively — the
completed expressivity suite of Section 7 is the ﬁrst direct test, on the trained 150 B pair. On this
pair, GDN2 leads long-horizon S5 tracking and E97 leads the modular-quadratic task under their
respective protocols. Further scale/budget persistence is falsiﬁed if that map gap closes under a
common optimization protocol.
• Emender ﬁne-tuned on reasoning benchmarks separates from GDN2 on tasks requiring multi-step
state-tracking. The Section 7 QA panels at the predecessor cohort’s training budget sit below the
threshold where reasoning benchmarks diﬀerentiate. After targeted ﬁne-tuning on a reasoning suite
(BIG-Bench Hard, ReCLor, FOLIO, multi-step object tracking), the Emender is expected to separate
from the linear baseline speciﬁcally on items where multi-step preﬁx tracking is load-bearing (object
tracking, formal fallacies, multi-step entailment). Falsiﬁed if E97 and GDN2 remain within standard
error on state-tracking-shaped subsets after matched ﬁne-tuning.
• PNR update-rule variants are per-step-body edits under the multi-programmed kernel. The fused
forward/backward Triton kernel (Section 5) is parameterized by the update map and the gating.
A diﬀerent bounded PNR update rule at the same matrix-state signature should be expressible
41
by editing the per-step body, without satisfying an associative-scan or chunkwise-WY compatibility
constraint and with the multi-programmed time loop unchanged. Falsiﬁed if a representative variant
(e.g., decoupled erase/write gates in the style of Gated DeltaNet-2 (Hatamizadeh et al., 2026), or a
higher-order delta with two-step memory) requires kernel rewrites beyond the per-step body.
• Emender combined with hybrid attention should outperform either alone. The Section 7 hybrid-
degradation result (Emender/GDN AABB underperforms either pure family on state tracking at
8 M) is read here as a property of the Emender/GDN pairing speciﬁcally. The prediction is that
an Emender-with-attention hybrid (Emender layers interleaved with attention layers, in the style of
M2
RNN’s hybrid conﬁguration) outperforms both pure Emender and pure attention on combined
state-tracking and long-range mixing benchmarks at matched parameter and token budgets. Falsiﬁed
if the hybrid underperforms either constituent at matched budget on the combined benchmark.
• A broadened CMA-ES sweep places the head-count interior optimum above the predecessor’s H =
370. The predecessor-cohort searches (Section A.4) arrived at H = 370 for E88 after successive
bound repositionings against persistent upward pressure from the optimizer. The conjecture is that
the same fairness search under broader bounds, subject only to the kernel-occupancy ceiling of the
multi-programmed substrate, returns an interior optimum at or above that band on the Emender
at 1.3 B (E97’s own search selected H = 216 within its conﬁgured bounds); H ∼ 1000 is the next
bracket worth testing. Falsiﬁed if a broadened CMA-ES sweep under matched per-token compute
returns an interior optimum at or below the current selection.
Some of these will falsify; the falsiﬁcations are the next round of evidence.
13 Future Work
A matched 3–7 B E97/GDN2 study would test training viability, memory cost, and computational behavior
across scale. The feedback, optimization, and driven-dynamics tasks in Section 8 would test utility beyond
language. Mathematical targets are a recurrent tanh tracker (O1), constrained aﬃne/reﬂection realizability
(G3), production-range persistence, and ﬁnite-precision robustness.
The results above leave several directions open. The ﬁrst few bear directly on the empirical comparison
of Section 6 and Section 7 the formal targets that follow are the open ends of the Lean development in
Section 4.
A stronger raw-write baseline
The within-class ordering reported here — the Emender ahead of the raw-write update M2
RNN-CMA at
the 8 M probes — is conditional on the no-tuning probe protocol, and the raw-write family is not a 1.3
B arm (Section 1). One contrast in the current evidence stays entangled because of that: the gap reﬂects
either a genuine diﬀerence in what the two update rules express or the residue of a raw-write conﬁguration
that received less eﬀective optimization. The way to separate the two is a stronger raw-write baseline —
a matched state-tracking search on the raw-write family at the 8 M probe shape, and a 1.3 B raw-write
arm once its kernels run on this stack — which distinguishes the conditional reading ("under the eﬀort used
here") from the family-level reading ("under any matched optimization"). This experiment would locate the
Emender-versus-raw-write gap, and Section 10 names it as the load-bearing follow-up.
Scale beyond 1.3 B
The matched-token loss parity is measured at the 1.3 B class on The Pile, at a single 30-GPU-day training
extent per arm. This is the named scale limitation of Section 10: whether the parity holds at larger parameter
counts, and whether the state-tracking separation grows or vanishes with scale, is open. The load-bearing
experiment is a matched 3–7 B E97/GDN2 pair under the same recipe; the single-arm E97 513 B-token
Frontier continuation that exists cannot answer a paired question, and a corpus move (e.g. to a license-clean
42
stream) would need its own matched pair. Section 12 states the throughput-scaling form of this question as
a falsiﬁable prediction.
Additional seeds and architecture-internal revalidation
Each 1.3 B run is a multi-week trajectory per architecture, so additional seeds at this band are a multi-
week investment apiece; the 1.3 B comparison is single-seed per architecture for that reason, and a seed-
averaged repeat is open. Several architecture-internal choices in the Emender — the output gate, the state
non-linearity (tanh versus linear), and the decay parameterization (simple sigmoid versus Mamba2-style
log-space) — tie on loss at small scale; revalidating each at 1.3 B is open.
A capacity bound on the S5 generators themselves
The k-step separation proved in Section 4 (emender_m2rnn_k_step_separation, set C′
) shows that for every
ﬁnite k the gap between the delta-correcting and raw-write updates persists, but it runs on a constructed
two-dimensional witness alphabet rather than on the S5 generators. The natural next-paper target is a
capacity bound stated directly on the S5 generator alphabet: for any ﬁxed-weight raw-write recurrence at
state dimension d, an explicit length T(d) past which tracking the S5 cosets becomes unreachable, while the
Emender at the same d still tracks them. That bound needs a bounded-precision raw-write class and Merrill-
style state-counting lower bounds not yet available in Mathlib. The Section 7 length-extrapolation curves on
parity, ﬁnite-state tracking, and the modular counter show positive but non-monotone gaps. For parity the
gap peaks near 0.359 at T = 320, then narrows to about 0.337 at T = 500. These are retained-advantage
observations, not evidence of the proposed S5 capacity bound or of a uniformly widening gap.
A partial order on PNR update rules
The ordering established here, delta-correcting above raw-write, is a noncontainment witness between two
matched-signature update families, not a proved strict set-inclusion order on the PNR class (emender_m2rn
n_one_step_resource_separation_embeds, set C in Section 4). Whether that relation extends to a partial
order over the broader PNR space — whether antisymmetry and transitivity hold across the full space, under
an explicit common resource class — is open. The horizon is the maximal element: which PNR update rule
carries the highest one-step expressive power for its compute class. The RecurrentResourceFormalism,
the Lean signature in which the Section 4 separations are stated, is the tool for climbing that order. More
ﬂexible bounded update rules are the natural rungs, among them split erase/write variants in the style of
Gated DeltaNet-2 (Hatamizadeh et al., 2026); placing them in the order is open work.
Lifting the latching theorems to the whole layer
The Section 4 latching set establishes the slot-level half of the Emender primitive: three theorems covering
saturation insensitivity, default hold under a bounded delta, and release on a suﬃcient counter-delta. Two
formal targets sit on top of it. The ﬁrst is a formalism reﬁnement — an architecture-level lift that promotes
the slot-wise latching theorems to the RecurrentResourceFormalism.MemorySemantics.latchAttractor
label on the Emender signature in the RecurrentResourceFormalism, recasting slot-level behavior as a
statement about the matrix-state attractor structure of the whole layer. The second is a Lean-targeted
theorem that connects Section 4 to the Section 7 empirics: a basin-survival bridge that takes an S5 coset in a
future recurrent realization (ﬁrst closing O1) and shows it survives an adversary whose per-slot perturbation
budget stays below the Section 4 release threshold, lifting the slot-level guarantees to a coset-level guarantee
on the state-tracking task.

A.1 Predecessor-cohort systems measurements
A Lean 4 deﬁnition (emender_1p27B) pins the E88 predecessor counts: twelve layers of 370 heads, each with
a 32 × 32 tile, yielding 22,200 independent recurrent programs per token at batch size 5 and 370 × (32 × 32)
recurrent-state scalars per layer.
Driving the 1.273 B E88 predecessor instance (batch 5, context 2048) through its own training path on
a free NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada (48 GB) and sampling nvidia-smi once a second over a sustained post-
warmup window, the GPU holds median 100% utilization (mean 99.8%, minimum 96% across 133 samples)
while drawing 97% of its 300 W power cap. The accelerator stays busy on width-axis multi-programming
alone, with no time-axis scan. Sustained throughput is 7,492 tokens/s (mean over steady 50-step windows;
median 7,468); a slower sibling GPU sustained 7,277 tokens/s, so the absolute ﬁgure is GPU-dependent at
the few-percent level. E97 uses the shared E88-derived Triton engine with split-edit inputs, with occupancy
measured here for the E88 geometry; E97 occupancy proﬁling remains pending.
Occupancy and peak-arithmetic utilization measure diﬀerent quantities. Model-FLOPs utilization (MFU)
— computed with the standard 6N convention against the card’s dense bf16 peak of 364 TFLOPS — is
15.7%, a lower bound: 6N counts only the weight-matmul FLOPs and excludes the recurrence and gate
operations, so the true MFU is slightly higher. High occupancy with modest MFU is the expected proﬁle
of a model bound by memory bandwidth and the serial recurrence rather than by arithmetic: the GPU is
busy nearly all of the time but converts about 16% of its peak FLOPs into useful work.
What that throughput buys on the width axis is best read against a real time-axis kernel. A chunkwise
linear-scan baseline — Gated DeltaNet as implemented in the ﬂash-linear-attention (FLA) library (1.352 B,
the CMA-matched shape of the predecessor cohort), run on the same GPU, context, and time budget —
sustains 8,248 tokens/s at MFU 18.4% and the same near-100% occupancy. The E88 predecessor reaches ≈
91% of that linear-scan kernel’s tokens/s (7,492 / 8,248) without performing the sequential time-axis scan at
all. Width-axis multi-programming therefore recovers linear-scan-class throughput on the width axis. FLA
is about 10% faster here, at a slightly larger parameter count and a diﬀerent batch; the measured standing
is parity-class.

A.3 Lineage of the E63 → E88 → E97 experimental program
The architecture presented in Section 3 is the endpoint of a multi-year ablation program, and this appendix
records how its 1.3 B instances — E88 ﬁrst, E97 in the body of the paper — were arrived at: which design
choice was settled at which stage, and what each one ﬁxed. The design history documents the measurements
behind the choices in Section 3. The milestone labels (E63, E70–E75, E88, E97) are internal codenames
marking stages of that program; they carry no meaning beyond their ordering; E88 appears in the body
only as the developmental predecessor of E97. The reference implementation of the 1.3 B stack, including
its fused Triton forward and backward recurrence kernels, is released publicly (see the availability statement
in Section 11).
• Where the nonlinearity goes (E63). Motivated by nonlinear recurrent computation (Siegelmann &
Sontag, 1995), this stage selected where the nonlinearity is placed: on the matrix state itself, rather
than only on the gates or the output. The Emender’s tanh on the state S is inherited from this
stage.
• The fused kernel and the gradient-spike ﬁx (E70–E75). Established the fused Triton kernel pattern
— outer product, decay, and tanh computed as a single kernel — and surfaced the failure mode
it had to survive: unbounded outer products spike the gradient. The L2
-normalized key write the
Emender uses is the ﬁx.
• The ﬁrst stable 1.3 B instance (E88). Stable at the billion-parameter class under schedule-free
AdamW. Its load-bearing parameterization choices, each ﬁxed by ablation: log-space Mamba2 decay
(with weight-decay exemption); L2
-normalized q, k; SiLU on input projections before normalization;
the numerically stable tanh(z) = 2σ(2z) − 1 in the fused kernel; output RMSNorm inside the layer
removed (−0.10 nats); short convolutions removed (−0.027 nats); no write gate; SiLU output gate.
• Why the M2
RNN baseline is reshaped (M2
RNN-CMA). The paper-default M2
RNN shape (dim =
3072, depth = 10, H = 759, N = 16) diverged at step 8,400 under the training setup of the

= 1920, depth = 21, H = 370, N = 16) is stable, and it is the M2

• The split-edit extension (E97). E97 extends the E88 cell with the two input-dependent split gates of

Per-architecture CMA-ES protocol
All three 1.3 B-class architectures (Emender, M2
RNN-CMA, Gated DeltaNet) received independent CMA-
ES (Hansen et al., 2003) hyperparameter and shape search over the same six knobs: width, depth, head count,
state width, output gating, learning rate. Each search ran under matched candidate budget: population 16,
ﬁxed wall-clock per candidate, identical ﬁtness rule of mean training nats over a ﬁxed late-training window.
When CMA-ES drifted onto a conﬁgured-range edge, the range was repositioned and search continued,
applied identically across the three architectures. The per-family winner shapes in the table above carry into
the Section 7 expressivity probes and Section 4 formal analysis unchanged. The concurrent M2
RNN paper
(Mishra et al. (Mishra et al., 2026), section 5.2) holds width, depth, optimizer, learning rate, weight decay
and gradient clipping uniform across compared architectures and varies only the sequence-mixing block, a
fair-by-uniformity protocol with shared hyperparameters rather than per-architecture selection.

A search conﬁgured for cross-architecture fairness thus placed its optimum at high head count without being
tuned toward it: throughput comes from many small, independently addressed recurrent programs.

Under the addressing-bottleneck hypothesis, the relevant geometry may be an addressing bottleneck rather
than a generic head-count eﬀect. The paper-default shape has high value-head multiplicity — many value

projections. The CMA reshape may reduce that bottleneck by moving toward address-program multiplicity:
more independently addressed recurrent programs, each with its own small state tile, rather than more
value channels behind the same address path. The stable M2

A.5 Held-out bits-per-byte measurement (predecessor cohort)
This appendix is the evidentiary backbone of the held-out descriptive closeness reported in Section A.4: it
documents exactly how the held-out bits-per-byte ﬁgures are measured and places them in a landscape of
reference models and classical compressors. Every number is measured by the eval harness described below;
none is re-ﬁtted. The measurement code, slice manifests, and re-exported checkpoints are public (see the
availability statement in Section 11).
Tokenizer-invariant held-out bpb
So that models with diﬀerent tokenizers are comparable on identical bytes, held-out bpb is computed from
each model’s real summed negative log-likelihood over the slice and the slice’s real UTF-8 byte count:
bpb = (total NLLnats/ ln 2)/total UTF-8 bytes.
Each model uses its own tokenizer, and the denominator is the shared byte count, so bytes/token is a measured
per-tokenizer quantity (4.00 for the GPT-NeoX BPE, 3.82 for our p50k_base runs on the canonical slice),
whereas the train-loss curve uses the ﬁxed bytes/token = 3.92 constant in Section 6. The held-out and
train-loss ﬁgures are therefore distinct objects: they diﬀer in normalization as well as in the data scored.
Every token is scored once under a sliding window (context 2048, stride 1024) with up to 2047 tokens of
left context, the standard ﬁxed-length-model perplexity recipe; out-of-distribution anchors GPT-2 XL and
OPT-1.3B run at context 1024 (GPT-2 XL position limit). Pipeline: scripts/measure_pile_bpb.py (open
models) and scripts/measure_pile_bpb_elman.py (our models, in-harness forward).
Algorithm 8 states the scoring order behind the held-out measurement. The denominator counts the shared
source bytes once; overlapping context is not rescored. Correct schedule-free weight recovery and the ﬁrst-
block sanity check precede reporting, and a failure is not a numeric measurement.
Algorithm 8 — tokenizer-invariant held-out evaluation.
Input: sha-verified slice bytes, model, tokenizer, window/stride
1. Load the bound inference weights; apply required y-mode recovery.
2. Tokenize the slice with this model’s tokenizer.
3. Run the first-block sanity gate; stop on failure.
4. Slide the context window; mask already scored target positions.
5. Sum next-token negative log-likelihood on newly scored positions.
6. Divide total NLL by log(2) and by the slice’s UTF-8 byte count.
Return: bits per byte, token/byte counts, slice and weight identities

Second distribution: Common Pile (contamination control)
Because the open Pile-trained references (Pythia, GPT-Neo) saw the Pile in training, their Pile numbers are
eﬀectively in-distribution. As an independent check we re-measure through the identical pipeline on a held-
out slice of Common Pile (v0.1 main-mix; 9,999,606 bytes, document-aligned on the 0x1E record separator,
at a random deep oﬀset 65.5% into its 1 TB corpus), a permissively-licensed corpus none of these models
trained on. The Pile-trained models score within ≤ 0.003 bpb of their Pile numbers there (Pythia-1.4B
+0.0028, GPT-Neo-1.3B +0.0002, Pythia-1B +0.0003), so the Pile result is not contamination-inﬂated. Our
three models on the same Common-Pile slice measure Emender 0.981, GDN 0.963, M2
RNN-CMA 0.973 —
within ≈0.02 bpb of their Pile ﬁgures, with ordering GDN < M2
RNN-CMA < Emender (again not the
train-loss ordering), consistent with a small distribution shift rather than a blow-up. Best classical coder on
Common Pile is xz −9 at 2.061 bpb.
